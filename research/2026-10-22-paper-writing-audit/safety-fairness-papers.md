## 1. Responsible Diffusion Models via Constraining Text Embeddings within Safe Regions

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Award_winners/Responsible_Diffusion_Models_via_Constraining_Text_Embeddings_within_Safe_Regions__2505_15427.pdf). This is an intervention-method paper: learn a small, global modification to the complete encoded prompt, then use it to suppress unsafe content or steer demographic attributes. Its evidence concerns generated-image classifier outcomes, distributional balance, and image quality; the paper does not establish a formally certified safe embedding region.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** The order is Introduction → Background and Related Work → Method → Experiments → Conclusion, with implementation, cost, hyperparameters, and additional experiments in Appendices A–C. The introduction does more than list prior methods: it groups defenses by intervention site and assigns each a failure mode—input filters miss implicit prompts, parameter changes can damage other knowledge, and denoising interventions add overhead. It then moves the intervention to the whole text embedding. Section 3.1 strengthens this transition by first trying the obvious alternative, subtracting two prompt embeddings; Figure 2 (p. 4) illustrates why that also changes unwanted attributes. The optimization-based direction is therefore introduced as a response to an identified failure, rather than as an unexplained architectural ingredient.

[Certain] **Mechanism and objective.** The frozen diffusion model supplies noise predictions for moving toward or away from a target concept. The learned prompt offset minimizes squared disagreement between its own induced noise prediction and that target across denoising steps (Eq. 7–8, p. 4). The offset is factorized as a rank-one product, reducing the SD-v1.4 trainable representation to 845 parameters (Section 3.2; Appendix A.3). Safety uses a direction away from unsafe concepts. Fair generation learns attribute-specific directions and samples them uniformly—for example, male/female—before adding them to the prompt embedding (Section 3.3, pp. 5–6).

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Table 1 (p. 7) tests general unsafe-content reduction on I2P, reporting an overall detected-inappropriate fraction of 0.12 versus 0.40 for original SD. Table 2 tests Ring-A-Bell adversarial prompts, providing a distinct stress condition rather than repeating ordinary prompts. Table 3 (p. 8) evaluates demographic balance across 36 professions and normal/“successful” prompt templates. Table 4 tests composition with existing defenses; its SDXL results require retraining the changed-size offset, unlike direct transfer into ESD/SLD (Section 4.3). Table 5 tests preservation with COCO FID and CLIP scores. Appendix C.1/Table 6 isolates rank-one versus full-offset parameterization; Appendix C.2 examines guidance start time; Appendix A.3 reports training cost. This makes each methodological promise answerable by a different experiment.

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** Figure 1 provides the geometric intuition; Figure 3 (p. 5) makes frozen versus trainable components and iterative updates visible; Figure 4 makes the low-rank design tangible; Figures 5–6 show output consequences. The strongest writing pattern is pairing every desirable intervention effect with a preservation test. “Fewer unsafe outputs” alone could be achieved by destroying generation quality; the separate fidelity section addresses that obvious objection.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** Author-stated qualifications include prompt-dependent warm-up sensitivity (Appendix C.2), retraining for other encoder dimensions (Section 4.3), and weakening individual effects when vectors are combined (Appendix C.7). [Likely] Reviewer inference: classifier-defined safety and uniform CLIP-predicted binary-gender/three-race proportions are operational targets, not complete definitions of safety or fairness. The remaining adversarial nudity fraction, 0.316 in Table 2, also prevents reading “safe region” as a universal guarantee. The conclusion’s assertion of unaffected other semantics exceeds the finite quality tests.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Existing interventions act at [location], where [specific failure] persists; our diagnostic shows that a naive move to [new location] also damages [property]. We therefore learn [restricted intervention] and evaluate its intended effect, adversarial boundary, preservation of normal behavior, and deployment cost separately.”

## 2. Before It’s Too Late: A State Space Model for the Early Prediction of Misinformation and Disinformation Engagement

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Web4Good_special_track/Before_It_s_Too_Late_A_State_Space_Model_for_the_Early_Prediction_of_Misinformation_and_Disinf__2502_04655.pdf). IC-Mamba is a temporal prediction method with post-level and opinion-level tasks. It predicts engagement and predefined opinion/narrative labels on the studied datasets; it does not demonstrate an intervention that prevents misinformation or discover the truth of arbitrary claims.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** Introduction → Related Work → IC-Mamba → Experiments and Results → Conclusion, followed by ethics, settings, notation, and additional forecasts. The introduction starts with a concrete societal trajectory, QAnon, and narrows the operational question from “could harm have been anticipated?” to forecasting engagement with emerging opinions. Its technical bottleneck is then made visible: platforms provide cumulative counts at irregular observation times rather than exact action times. Figure 1 (p. 2) translates that access constraint into the interval-censoring problem. Three RQs organize the promised contribution: model irregular dynamics, predict within early observation windows, and update forecasts of opinions as data arrives. Table 1 situates prior work by engagement channels and platforms, not only by model names.

[Certain] **Mechanism and objective.** Sections 3.2–3.4 combine relative/absolute temporal encodings, content and user representations, interval-length information, and a Mamba-style state update whose transition depends on elapsed time. In plain terms, the model is told both what engagement was observed and how long the unobserved gap lasted. Pretraining combines next-engagement squared error with a temporal-state coherence penalty (Eq. 1–2, pp. 4–5). The two-tier construction first summarizes individual posts, then models the chronological arrivals of posts within an opinion (Section 3.6/Figure 3, p. 5). This provides a reason for having both post-level and collective evaluations.

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Section 4.2 (p. 6) specifies chronological 70/15/15 train/validation/test splits. Table 3 (p. 7) compares engagement forecasting against eight baselines across four SocialSense themes and DiN, while the text/user/time removals put component evidence beside the main scores. Table 4 (p. 8) evaluates the separate classification claim. Figure 5a varies observation time from 15 minutes to six hours, directly addressing early prediction. Figures 5b–c use seven- and ten-day windows for an opinion-level monitoring example; Section 4.5 explicitly says forecasts incorporate new observations as time advances. Appendix D (pp. 10–11) separates fixed-window and early/mid/late next-point forecasts. Appendix B reports averaging across ten seeds.

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** The figures form a scale ladder: observation process (Figure 1), individual architecture (Figure 2), hierarchical architecture (Figure 3), empirical data distributions (Figure 4), then forecasting behavior (Figure 5). The useful writing lesson is to preserve the observation-window/prediction-horizon distinction throughout the paper. Each RQ has an identifiable experiment, and the opinion-level claim requires an additional architectural and evaluation layer rather than being inferred automatically from post accuracy.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** Author-stated future work includes cross-platform modeling and real-time deployment; evaluation excludes posts with fewer than four engagement intervals (p. 5). [Likely] Reviewer inference: a dynamic single-opinion illustration is narrower evidence than prospective deployment across unseen campaigns. Pretraining is described over the 1.78-million-post corpus (Section 3.5), but its separation from temporal holdouts is not explicit; this is a provenance question, not demonstrated leakage. [Certain] Two source inconsistencies require care when copying result language: Table 3 shows TH outperforming IC-TH although adjacent prose says the reverse; Figure 5a visually places 15-minute error near 0.34, whereas the abstract associates 0.118–0.143 with 15–30 minutes. The main text associates 0.118 with six hours. Both were checked in rendered pages.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “The operational setting exposes [irregular/incomplete observations], so the prediction problem must preserve [time and granularity constraints]. We introduce [representation and transition changes], then answer separate questions about overall accuracy, earliest usable observation window, and collective forecasting under incoming data.”

## 3. Cross-Modal Transfer from Memes to Videos: Addressing Data Scarcity in Hateful Video Detection

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Web4Good_special_track/Cross_Modal_Transfer_from_Memes_to_Videos_Addressing_Data_Scarcity_in_Hateful_Video_Detection__2501_15438.pdf). This is a data-transfer and annotation-method paper using existing VLMs, rather than a new model-architecture paper. Its central contribution is the argument that label alignment allows accessible meme data to substitute for, or augment, scarce video training data. The supplied version contains residual ACM template metadata; its typography is therefore not a reliable submission-format template.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** Introduction → Related Work → Methodology → Experiments → Conclusion. The introduction has explicit Motivation, Research Objectives, and Contributions paragraphs. It quantifies the resource imbalance—roughly one thousand annotated videos versus substantially larger meme resources—then proposes transfer using shared visual/textual information. Crucially, it reports that naive meme fine-tuning was limited by incompatible label definitions. The proposed repair is therefore not simply “more training examples”: re-annotate examples according to the target task’s meaning. The two experimental RQs preserve two different practical scenarios: replacement when video labels are scarce, and supplementation when some are available (Section 4, p. 5).

[Certain] **Mechanism and objective.** MHC’s hateful/offensive categories are merged for binary classification, whereas HateMM retains its hateful/non-hateful definition (Section 3.1). The relabeling pipeline combines original labels with VLM predictions prompted using labeled video examples; humans adjudicate disagreements, and the final label follows majority voting (Section 3.2/Figure 2). LoRA fine-tuning then uses the target label in a sequence cross-entropy objective (Section 3.3, p. 5). LLaMA-3.2-11B receives one sampled video frame, while LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7B receives sixteen; meme augmentation creates sixteen transformed still-image frames for the latter. These details define what “cross-modal” means here: transfer of text–image evidence, without a learned audio channel.

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Table 1 (p. 4) explores demonstration count for the relabeling model. Figures 3–4 show how label distributions change under target definitions. Table 2 (p. 6) varies training-set size alongside estimated annotation time: 3,000 relabeled FHM memes are assigned 10.5 hours versus 53.3 hours for 800 MHC videos. Table 3 (p. 7) contrasts no tuning, video tuning, original memes, and relabeled memes across two VLMs and two video benchmarks; this directly tests both transfer and the value of relabeling. Table 4 (p. 8) adds the combined video-plus-meme condition, reporting macro-F1 0.81/0.82 on MHC/HateMM versus 0.78/0.81 for video-only tuning. Section 4.4 also counts corrected and newly introduced errors. Table 5 supplies concrete borderline cases.

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** Figure 1 positions the alternative training routes; Figure 2 makes the human intervention point visible, rather than hiding it behind “automatic relabeling.” Distribution plots diagnose label mismatch; the strategy tables separate the proposed causes of improvement. The strongest reusable structure is resource bottleneck → naive transfer failure → targeted data repair → replacement and augmentation contrasts → annotation-cost accounting. It demonstrates how a data-centric contribution can sustain a full paper without adding model blocks.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** The authors explicitly mention possible but unconfirmed HateMM contamination in the pretrained VLM (p. 4), exclusion of audio (p. 3), and performance plateaus (pp. 7–8). A more direct protocol limitation is explicit in Section 4.1.3 (p. 6): the best epoch is chosen on the video test set. [Likely] Reviewer inference: reported performance consequently is not an untouched-holdout estimate; validation-based selection is the pattern to copy instead. Also, “no video training data” should not be stretched to “no labeled video information,” because relabeling uses labeled video demonstrations. Four selected cases illustrate behavior but cannot establish generalization.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “The target modality is expensive to label, while a cheaper source shares [useful information] but differs in [task semantics]. We isolate that mismatch with an original-versus-realigned data comparison, then evaluate substitution, supplementation, and annotation cost as separate claims.”

## 4. Modality Interactive Mixture-of-Experts for Fake News Detection

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Web4Good_special_track/Modality_Interactive_Mixture_of_Experts_for_Fake_News_Detection__2501_12431.pdf). MIMoE-FND is a multimodal routing-method paper. It proposes a four-way operational taxonomy based on semantic alignment and agreement between unimodal predictions, then uses that taxonomy to supervise routing into fusion experts. Its demonstrated target is benchmark fake-news classification, rather than measured protection of vulnerable communities.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** Introduction → Related Work → Method → Experiments → Conclusion; detailed setup and complexity are moved to appendices. The introduction challenges a specific simplification: text–image semantic consistency alone cannot determine how the modalities should be fused. Figure 1 (p. 2) is integral to the reasoning, showing Agreed Misalignment, Agreed Alignment, Disagreed Misalignment, and Disagreed Alignment. This creates a vocabulary that reappears in the method, expert names, losses, and case studies. Related work then distinguishes general modality-interaction research from prior fake-news fusion methods, narrowing the claimed novelty rather than implying that mixture-of-experts itself is new.

[Certain] **Mechanism and objective.** BERT and MAE encode text and images; improved MoE blocks refine text, image, and concatenated representations. Jensen–Shannon divergence between auxiliary unimodal prediction distributions measures agreement, while CLIP cosine similarity supplies semantic alignment (Section 3.3, p. 5). Thresholded combinations create four supervision labels for the interaction gate. The highest-scoring route activates a fusion expert. The objective combines final binary classification, auxiliary unimodal classification, and gate-supervision/regularization terms (Eq. 8, p. 6). Thus the experts are specialized according to model-derived interaction proxies; there is no independently human-labeled interaction taxonomy in the described evaluation.

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Table 1 (p. 6) compares Weibo, GossipCop, and Weibo-21 accuracy and class-specific precision/recall/F1. Accuracy is 0.928, 0.895, and 0.956, with GossipCop tying BMR at the displayed precision. Table 2 (p. 7) tests unimodal baselines and removal of gate regularization, semantic supervision, prediction-agreement supervision, or both interaction targets. This table addresses the central question: does explicit interaction supervision help beyond the fusion architecture? Figure 3 varies gate-loss weight and gate learning rate; Appendix B extends that sensitivity analysis. Figure 4 (p. 8) follows four routed examples through unimodal predictions, dispatch weights, and final predictions. Appendix D/Table 3 (p. 12) reports a 16-sample inference-time comparison and derives the architecture’s scaling cost.

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** Figure 2 (p. 4) separates feature refinement, gate supervision, and final detection, while placing the reusable iMoE block in its own inset. Its boxes therefore correspond to actual methodological subproblems. Figure 4 closes the explanatory loop by reusing the introduction’s four interaction labels in observed outputs. The strongest writing move is taxonomy → operational measurements → routing architecture → ablations that remove each source of supervision. This makes a complex model easier to justify than a sequence of unexplained components.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** Author-stated qualifications include weaker gains and routing sensitivity on GossipCop, imbalanced expert training, and added inference cost (Sections 4.1–4.3; Appendices B/D). Appendix A.2 (p. 11) reports the best test accuracy from 50 epochs with one fixed seed. [Likely] Reviewer inference: this selection rule limits clean holdout interpretation, and four successful routing examples are illustrative interpretability evidence, not a causal test of what the experts learned. [Certain] The printed balance-loss formula in Eq. 6 (p. 5), visually checked, sums normalized probabilities minus a uniform distribution without absolute values or squares; as written, it sums to zero. That equation needs reconciliation with implementation before reusing its optimization argument.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “A single fusion assumption conflates [two distinct properties], producing [enumerated interaction cases]. We operationalize those properties, route instances to appropriate computation, and test the value of each supervision signal against the same architecture without it.”

## 5. Unmasking Gender Bias in Recommendation Systems and Enhancing Category-Aware Fairness

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Web4Good_special_track/Unmasking_Gender_Bias_in_Recommendation_Systems_and_Enhancing_Category_Aware_Fairness__2502_17921.pdf). This is primarily a measurement paper with a mitigation extension. Its contribution is category-aware comparison of recommendation exposure across gender groups, followed by optimization of one proposed metric. Fairness is operationalized as similarity of category distributions between male and female users, rather than established by matching utility alone.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** Introduction → Related Work → Proposed Evaluation Metrics → Genre-Aware Regularization → Experiments → Analysis of Proposed Metrics → Discussion and Conclusion. The unusually useful opening move is Figure 1 (p. 2): similar Precision@10 can coexist with different action/romance exposure. The introduction turns this into a measurement blind spot before proposing a remedy. Related work separates gender fairness, fairness definitions, and consumer-side evaluation; Section 3.1 then uses two users with mixed genre preferences to explain what aggregate utility can miss. This gives the equations a stated measurement purpose. Only after defining the metrics does Section 4 ask whether one can be used as a training objective.

[Certain] **Mechanism and objective.** Six category-sensitive measures are introduced: Category Coverage, Relative Category Representation, Category Mean Average Precision, Category Discounted Cumulative Gain, Category Mean Reciprocal Rank, and Category RPrecision (Sections 3.3–3.4, pp. 4–5). Multi-category items contribute fractional category weights; the measures differ in their normalization or ranking discounts. The Gender Balance Score sums the absolute male–female gaps across categories (Eq. 7–8). A Category-Coverage-based gap becomes a fairness regularizer mixed with the recommendation loss by alpha (Eq. 9–10). Section 5.3 states that custom differentiable top-k category-distribution implementations are used; it is therefore inaccurate to infer that the authors simply backpropagate through an unmodified hard ranking.

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Figure 1 motivates why the new measurement is needed; Appendix A.4 (p. 12) documents balanced sampling of male/female test users for that illustration. Table 2 (p. 6) reports ordinary recommendation quality and all six gap measures for five base models across MovieLens-100K, MovieLens-1M, and Yelp, including regularized MF/VAE-CF/NeuMF variants. Figure 2 (p. 7) disaggregates representative categories; Figure 3 (p. 8) shows within-model changes after regularization. Table 3/Figure 4 compare against two fairness-aware baselines, using MF as a common foundation. Appendix A.1/Figure 5 (p. 11) varies alpha and plots NDCG together with Category-Coverage disparity, making the optimization trade-off visible. The split is stated as user-based 70/10/20 (Section 5.1).

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** The manuscript builds a measurement argument through repeated changes of resolution: aggregate quality → category-specific disparity → aggregate multi-category scores → per-category residuals. The strongest transferable feature is the separation of metric construction from intervention. This lets the paper argue that existing evaluation can hide something even before arguing that its particular regularizer is best. Joint quality/fairness reporting also makes explicit that lowering a fairness score has a recommendation cost.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** The authors explicitly restrict the study to binary genders, acknowledge personalization tension, discuss differences in baseline training losses, and propose future multivalued-attribute extensions (Sections 3.1, 6.2, 7). [Likely] Reviewer inference: category-parity disparity is a chosen fairness criterion; a nonzero gap alone does not identify discrimination or distinguish legitimate preferences from stereotyping. Optimizing a proposed score demonstrates controllability, not by itself the score’s construct validity. [Certain] “Minimal performance loss” also needs condition-specific wording: Table 2, visually checked, shows Yelp MF NDCG falling from 0.1209 to 0.0773 and NeuMF from 0.1794 to 0.1124. Those cells should accompany any general trade-off claim.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “The prevailing metric treats [two outputs] as similar even though they differ in [decision-relevant distribution]. We define that missing quantity, show what it reveals across existing systems, and separately test whether optimizing it improves the selected criterion at an explicitly measured utility cost.”

## 6. Evaluating Robustness of LLMs on Crisis-Related Microblogs across Events, Information Types, and Linguistic Features

[Certain] **Source and contribution.** [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Web4Good_special_track/Evaluating_Robustness_of_LLMs_on_Crisis_Related_Microblogs_across_Events_Information_Types_and__2412_10413.pdf). This is a measurement/benchmark study, not a new-model paper. The supplied arXiv v1 is a single-column document marked “Preprint. Under review.” Its formatting should not be treated as a Web Conference camera-ready template. “Robustness” here largely means performance across naturally occurring data slices and prompting conditions, not controlled adversarial perturbation experiments.

[Certain] **Argument construction.** Introduction → Related Work → Assessment Methodology → Results and Discussion → Ethical Considerations → Conclusion/Future Work, with class results in Appendix A. The opening identifies an operational tension: disaster microblogs contain useful requests but overwhelming noise; supervised filtering can require new labels when events or categories change. The gap is therefore a missing assessment of whether off-the-shelf LLM generalization actually handles crisis-specific variation. Section 3 turns that broad question into four axes: disaster type, information type, country-language grouping, and linguistic features. The results subsections follow those axes directly, then add event-level and overall benchmarking. A measurement contribution thus gets an organizing logic without inventing an unnecessary architecture.

[Certain] **Design in plain language.** The study uses HumAID’s test split, reported as 15,160 tweets, drops the “other relevant information” category, and evaluates six specified proprietary/open models on nine information categories (Section 3.1, p. 3). It varies zero/one/three/five/ten examples per class, samples demonstrations from training data, and fixes temperature to zero; Llama-2’s ten-shot condition is excluded for context-length reasons (Sections 3.2/4.1). The common prompt includes category definitions (p. 4). For linguistic analysis, logistic regression predicts whether each classification is correct from a reduced feature set after examining correlations (Section 4.4, pp. 7–8). This is an association analysis, not an intervention on the text.

[Certain] **Claim-to-evidence chain.** Figure 1 (p. 3) exposes sample imbalance before reporting performance. Figure 2 (p. 4) crosses disaster types with models and shot counts. Figures 3–4 (p. 5) move from class scores to confusion matrices; the latter identifies urgent-needs messages being predicted as donation/rescue efforts. Figure 5 (p. 6) compares country groups. Figures 6–7 and Tables 1–2 map linguistic-feature distributions and regression coefficients; Figure 8 stratifies by hashtag position. Figure 9 (p. 8) supplies event-level coverage, while Figure 10/Table 3 (p. 9) supply aggregate comparisons. The cited supervised RoBERTa benchmark is 0.78 macro-F1, above the reported best LLM aggregate, GPT-4o zero-shot at 0.762. That retained comparator prevents the paper from equating newer models with universal superiority.

[Likely] **Figures and transferable strength.** The paper’s strongest writing feature is question-indexed disaggregation: each broad capability claim is unpacked into a slice where it can fail. The confusion matrices turn “low F1” into a specific operational error. The full event matrix supports breadth, while regression attempts to characterize correlates of failure. Copy that division of labor; the dense radar plots themselves are less straightforward to compare than aligned axes would be.

[Certain] **Boundaries.** The authors leave explanations of disaster/category weaknesses to future qualitative work and propose extending beyond text (p. 10). [Likely] Reviewer inference: country of an event does not establish each author’s native language, and natural feature associations do not establish typo or hashtag causality. [Certain] Source consistency also needs checking before quoting headline findings: the introduction says all GPT urgent-needs F1 scores are below 0.60, but Appendix A/Table 4 (p. 12, visually checked) reports GPT-4o values 0.70–0.85. Typo coefficients have p=0.113/0.077 in Tables 1–2, so those regressions do not establish a conventional 0.05-significant typo effect. These caveats constrain claims without negating the useful evaluation structure.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Broad claims of model generalization leave [operationally important dimensions] untested. We define those dimensions before evaluation, report aggregate and disaggregated performance, and use error analysis to distinguish observed failure patterns from explanations that remain untested.”

## Cross-paper writing lessons

[Likely] The six papers support three different middle structures, not one mandatory outline. An intervention method needs mechanism → intended effect → preservation → boundary, as Responsible Diffusion illustrates. A measurement paper needs blind spot → definition → validation or disaggregation → implication, as the gender and crisis studies illustrate. A data-transfer paper needs resource bottleneck → incompatibility → repair → matched contrasts, as Cross-Modal Transfer illustrates. IC-Mamba and MIMoE add useful organizing devices—RQs and a four-case taxonomy—that make their components and experiments easier to justify.

[Certain] Their strongest figures have different jobs: IC-Mamba Figure 1 explains the observation process; Gender Figure 1 exposes a metric blind spot; MIMoE Figure 1 defines cases; Cross-Modal Figure 2 shows human adjudication; Responsible Diffusion Figure 3 explains optimization; Crisis Figure 4 exposes error types. [Likely] A figure should be assigned one of these jobs before drawing it. A polished architecture diagram cannot replace a motivating diagnostic, and a case study cannot replace an aggregate experiment.

[Likely] The practical writing test is whether each introduction claim has a distinct evidential destination. “Works better,” “works earlier,” “preserves quality,” “transfers,” “saves annotation,” and “improves fairness” require different comparisons. These papers are most instructive where they keep those comparisons separate. Their weaknesses are also instructive: test-based model selection must not become a copied convention; broad societal benefit must remain an intended consequence unless measured; figures and appendices must agree with headline numbers; and an operational fairness or safety score must not silently become a universal ethical guarantee.

[Certain] No acceptance scores, independent reproduction claims, or new project proposals are implied by this analysis. The useful evidence here is how the supplied documents construct and support their arguments, including where their wording outruns their own reported tests.
