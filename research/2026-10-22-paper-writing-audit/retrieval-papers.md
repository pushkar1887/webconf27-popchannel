## 1. HtmlRAG

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is a representation-and-pipeline paper: preserve HTML as retrieved knowledge, then make that representation practical through cleaning and hierarchical pruning. Its central object is the document representation entering a RAG reader, rather than a newly pretrained reader. [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/HtmlRAG_HTML_is_Better_Than_Plain_Text_for_Modeling_Retrieved_Knowledge_in_RAG_Systems__2411_02959.pdf), Abstract and §3, pp. 1–3.

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Related Works (RAG; post-retrieval processing; structured-data understanding) → Methodology (problem definition; cleaning; block-tree construction; embedding and generative pruning) → Experiments (datasets; metrics; baselines; settings; main results; further analysis) → Conclusion and Future Works → acknowledgments/references → Appendices A–F.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** The introduction narrows from LLM factual shortcomings to web-based RAG, then identifies a routine preprocessing choice—HTML-to-text conversion—as a source of lost structure. Figure 1 provides a concrete PyTorch documentation example: table relations and semantic tags disappear. The proposed remedy immediately creates a second problem: raw HTML is long and noisy. That problem motivates cleaning. Cleaned HTML still exceeds useful budgets, motivating pruning; a fine-grained DOM is expensive, motivating blocks; isolated tiny blocks are difficult for embeddings, motivating a global generative pruner. These are successive problem–response pairs, not an unexplained list of modules (§1, pp. 1–2).

[Certain] **Mechanism.** Remove scripts/styles/comments and redundant structure; merge DOM fragments into adjustable blocks; remove blocks with low query–embedding similarity; then score finer block paths with a trained generative model and prune again. The generator scores valid paths through a token tree, skipping deterministic singleton branches and reusing prefixes (§3.2–3.4; Figures 2–4, pp. 3–6). The task objective is answer quality under a context-length budget, while path prediction supplies the pruner’s training signal.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Table 1 compares the complete pipeline against refiners using two Llama-3.1 reader sizes at 4K context, across six QA datasets with 400 sampled questions each (§4.1, §4.4). Table 2 isolates a different question: how cleaned HTML compares with raw HTML, plain text, and Markdown at 128K context. Table 3 removes the block tree and each pruning stage. Figure 5 varies block granularity, testing the rationale for coarse embedding selection followed by finer generative selection. Table 4 reports component costs; Appendix C/Table 5 measures answer-string retention during compression. The sequence moves from utility to representation choice to component necessity to operational cost.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** Use a first-page figure to expose information that the conventional pipeline discards. Then let the cost of your proposed remedy motivate the implementation. Figure 2 gives the processing route; Figure 3 explains the non-obvious scoring operation. Assigning those figures different explanatory jobs prevents the architecture diagram from carrying the entire argument.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** Appendix C acknowledges truncation-related loss even after nominally lossless cleaning; §4.6.4 offers embedding-only pruning for constrained settings. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** The title’s blanket superiority is broader than the tables: Table 1 includes losses on some 8B metrics, and Table 2 includes plain-text wins. Static web HTML, sampled QA questions, and two reader sizes bound the demonstrated setting. A successful compressed pipeline does not by itself isolate the causal contribution of HTML from its pruner.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “A routine transformation discards information the downstream task needs; demonstrate that loss with one recognizable example. Preserve that information, derive each processing stage from the resulting practical bottleneck, and evaluate representation, complete pipeline, components, and cost separately.”

## 2. ImageScope

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is a training-free task-unification and inference-orchestration paper. It maps text-to-image retrieval, composed image retrieval, and conversational image retrieval into a shared language-mediated retrieval interface, then refines candidates through local verification and overall evaluation. “Collective reasoning” denotes cooperating model roles, not a single newly trained architecture (Abstract; §3.1–3.2). [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/ImageScope_Unifying_Language_Guided_Image_Retrieval_via_Large_Multimodal_Model_Collective_Reas__2503_10166.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Related Work (language-guided retrieval; large models and reasoning) → Methodology (problem definition; unified framework; semantic synthesis; predicate verification; overall evaluation) → Experiments (setup; performance; ablation; efficiency/model-generality/qualitative discussion) → Conclusion → acknowledgments/references → Appendix A with parameter analysis, examples, prompts, and complete results.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** The paper first explains why text-to-image search evolved toward reference-image editing and multi-turn dialogue. Figure 1 makes those three interfaces concrete before arguing that isolated solutions fragment the task space. The insight is that language can serve as a compositional interface across these inputs. Three remaining failure modes then justify three stages: ambiguous instructions require semantic synthesis; missed fine details require proposition checks; reference-relative or holistic consistency requires overall evaluation (§1, pp. 1–3). The formal definition in §3.1 makes the unification precise through the same scoring function with different text/reference-image inputs.

[Certain] **Mechanism.** A captioner converts images to text. A reasoner decomposes feedback into addition/removal/modification/comparison/retention instructions, then creates three descriptions at different semantic granularities. Retrieval combines image-embedding and caption-embedding similarities. A verifier checks generated yes/no propositions for the top 20 candidates and sorts by satisfied-proposition count. An evaluator checks up to three leading candidates against the instruction and reference image (§3.3–3.5). Crucially, the final comparison is reference–candidate evaluation, not a tournament comparing candidate images against one another.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Table 2 evaluates composed retrieval on CIRCO, CIRR, and FashionIQ; Table 3 tests text-to-image retrieval on Flickr30K and MSCOCO; Figure 4/Appendix Table 6 tracks conversational retrieval through ten VisDial rounds. These are six datasets, with three FashionIQ subsets. Figure 3 adds stages sequentially to show where top-rank improvements arise. Figure 7 swaps reasoners and verifiers to support modular compatibility; Figure 5 and Appendix Tables 7–8 locate the inference bottleneck. Figure 6 shows instructions, generated propositions, and changing retrieval lists, exposing the intermediate behavior behind the final metric.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** Establish the input/task taxonomy visually before presenting the unified method. Keep stage names identical across introduction, pipeline, equations, ablations, and qualitative examples. Figure 2’s color-separated stages and worked image example make a complicated five-role system traceable; Appendix Figures 10–12 preserve the exact prompt interfaces rather than treating prompts as an invisible implementation detail.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** §3.4 explicitly relaxes strict conjunction because verifiers and images can be imperfect; §4.4.1 states that verification consumes most query time and trades off against candidate count. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** Stage gains support useful refinement, not logically sound verification or faithful reasoning. The reranker cannot recover candidates absent from its shortlist, and reported query latency does not account for all database-caption preparation. Table 2 shows competitive rather than universal FashionIQ dominance; Appendix Table 6 includes an initial-round Hits@1 deficit for the larger CLIP setting. There is no dedicated limitations section.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Define apparently separate tasks as different inputs to one interface, then explain the representation that makes their unification possible. Give each residual failure mode its own stage and validate task coverage, intermediate corrections, model substitutions, and runtime separately.”

## 3. LLM4Rerank

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is a prompt-controlled, multi-objective reranking framework. Its contribution is an extensible graph of aspect and control operations, with natural-language goals and a historical reranking pool; it does not introduce a learned guarantee for solving a multi-objective optimum (§2). [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/LLM4Rerank_LLM_based_Auto_Reranking_Framework_for_Recommendations__2406_12433.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Framework (problem formulation; overview; node construction; automatic reranking process) → Experiments (setup; overall performance/RQ1; aspect combination/RQ2; ablation/RQ3; case study) → Related Work → Conclusion → acknowledgments/references → Appendices A–F: algorithm, parameter analysis, inference, reproduction, adding nodes, future directions. Related work is deliberately deferred until the framework and evidence are available.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** It starts at the reranking stage, broadens the target from accuracy to diversity and fairness, then identifies three shortcomings: combining aspects, accommodating new requirements, and changing priorities after deployment. It argues that LLMs are especially suitable for short candidate lists, rather than entire catalogs. Nodes address extensibility; the “Goal” sentence and autonomous transitions address changing priorities; the history pool supports decisions across steps. The first-page “Relevance Statement” explicitly connects the work to user modeling, personalization, recommendation, and web applications (§1, pp. 1–2).

[Certain] **Mechanism.** Every run starts at Accuracy. Each aspect node prompts the same LLM using user features, the original candidate list, the Goal, and prior rerankings; it returns a new list and the next node name. Diversity and Fairness encode different list criteria. Backward removes the latest result; Stop returns the final history entry, with a visit-count cap providing another termination rule (§2.2–2.4). Figures 3–6 show node templates. Accuracy uses HR/NDCG; diversity uses α-NDCG; fairness uses group disparity in rank-derived scores, with dataset-specific group definitions (§3.1.3).

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Three datasets—ML-1M, KuaiRand, Douban-Movie—use the same GMF-generated 20-item candidates for competing rerankers (§3.1.1). Table 2 compares aspect-specific Goals and a balanced Goal with specialized and LLM baselines. Table 3 changes diversity/fairness priorities and reports both metrics and actual node/path frequencies; this directly tests whether the control sentence changes execution. Table 4 removes history, adaptive routing, or extra nodes. Figure 7 displays metric changes along common paths; it summarizes averages for those paths, rather than proving a single trajectory’s causal reasoning. Appendix B varies candidate count; Appendix C/Table 5 reports inference costs.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** A controllability claim needs behavioral evidence, not only a higher final score. Table 3 is the most reusable device: pair each requested priority with both the chosen computation and its measured consequence. Separate generic node interfaces from concrete prompt instances so that “extensible” has an inspectable meaning. The report’s RQs closely track its three headline promises.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** Appendix F acknowledges inference-speed limitations and difficulty handling longer contexts. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** The tests establish response to supplied Goal sentences, not improved satisfaction for real users. Different configurations win different objectives; one configuration does not simultaneously dominate everything. The selected fairness proxy covers defined item groups, not fairness in every normative sense. Performance differences from ablations do not establish that the generated reasoning is faithful. The visible placeholder publication metadata should not be copied as a formatting template.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Turn a vague requirement for personalization into an explicit control input and a visible decision interface. Then change that input experimentally and report both the altered execution path and the resulting objective tradeoffs.”

## 4. MA4DIV

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is an optimization-formulation paper applying cooperative multi-agent value learning to diversified ranking. Each document chooses a discrete ranking score simultaneously; the resulting list receives the actual diversity metric as reward. It replaces sequential document selection and hand-designed differentiable metric surrogates with a one-step joint-action formulation (§4.1–4.6). [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/MA4DIV_Multi_Agent_Reinforcement_Learning_for_Search_Result_Diversification__2403_17421.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Related Works (diversification; RL for IR; MARL) → Background (cooperative MARL; labeled diversification data) → The MA4DIV Model (elements; agent network; ranking; value decomposition; mixing/hypernetworks; training) → Experiments (three RQs; setup; public/industrial results; efficiency) → Conclusions and Future Work → references → Appendices A–G.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** The opening establishes that a document’s diversity contribution depends on preceding documents. It organizes prior work into greedy selection, single-agent RL, and simultaneous score-and-sort methods, then compares their weaknesses along effectiveness and efficiency. The proposed formulation maps directly onto those concerns: simultaneous actions reduce sequential ranking steps; a full ranking per episode supports exploration; metric reward avoids substituting a smoothed ranking objective. The final introduction also anticipates a data-scale objection by separating the small TREC benchmark from the larger industrial DU-DIV collection (§1, pp. 1–2).

[Certain] **Mechanism.** A shared agent network sees the query, its document, and cross-document features from self-attention. It chooses a discrete score through ε-greedy exploration. Sorting all scores creates a list whose α-NDCG is the common reward. A QMIX-style monotonic mixing network predicts joint value from individual action values; hypernetworks condition its weights on the state. Because the episode has one step, the TD target reduces to the observed reward, and replay permits reuse of sampled rankings (§4; Equations 5, 11, 14–15). Figure 1 connects the inference route to the separate training/value route; Algorithms 1–2 distinguish ranking from learning.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Table 2 compares six diversity metrics on TREC; Table 3 repeats them on DU-DIV. TREC contains 198 unique queries and is resampled into 6,232 candidate lists with query-based cross-validation; DU-DIV has 4,473 queries and 15 documents per query (§5.1; Appendix E). Figure 2 explicitly displays small-data overfitting. Table 4 compares training time on the same machine, and Figure 3 compares convergence against MDP-DIV. Appendix F explains replay-related sample reuse; Appendix G counts sequential inference stages. The reported public-data result is mixed, while DU-DIV leads across all six listed metrics.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** A taxonomy is useful when its axes become design requirements and experimental questions. The separate background section prevents the method from repeatedly explaining MARL vocabulary. The paper also exposes an inconvenient public-benchmark result before introducing industrial evidence; that is more informative than reporting only its favorable setting.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** §5.2.1 acknowledges overfitting on 198 queries, and the conclusion leaves general relevance/fairness ranking to future work. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** Using the true metric as reward does not prove global optimization: the value function is still learned under a factorization constraint. DU-DIV also changes representation and candidate-list length, so its advantage cannot be attributed to sample size alone. The appendix’s O(1) description counts sequential scoring steps, not total self-attention and sorting work. The text identifies a peak on test data around iteration 11; it does not clearly specify a separate checkpoint-selection policy, which is an unresolved reporting issue rather than established leakage.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Classify prior methods by the optimization compromise each makes, then formulate a decision process that changes those compromises explicitly. Evaluate objective quality and computational behavior separately, and preserve the difficult benchmark result when explaining the method’s operating conditions.”

## 5. PEAR

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is an intervention-based model adaptation paper with a tightly coupled efficiency claim: discover attention heads that hurt a synthetic copying task, learn scalar weights for those heads, and fold the weights into existing output projections before inference (§4). The supplied artifact is an October 2024 preprint, not an identified WWW camera-ready manuscript. [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/PEAR_Position_Embedding_Agnostic_Attention_Re_weighting_Enhances_Retrieval_Augmented_Generatio__2409_19745.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Related Works (context awareness; mechanistic interpretability) → Preliminaries: Discovery of Influential Attention Heads → Methodology (head discovery; coefficient learning) → Experiments (setup; RAG comparison; position-embedding applicability; knowledge preservation; K analysis; coefficient analysis) → Conclusion → references → Appendix A with head-discovery heatmaps.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** It starts with RAG’s dependence on usable context, introduces position-sensitive context failures, and separates prior remedies by their inference overhead and embedding-specific assumptions. Existing work on heads that suppress contextual copying then motivates a smaller intervention. The introduction previews both the random-repeat proxy and the re-weighting procedure, while the contributions foreground two measurable properties: zero additional inference computation and independence from a particular positional embedding (§1, pp. 1–3). Table 1’s repeated-letter example makes the otherwise abstract proxy task understandable before the full method appears.

[Certain] **Mechanism.** Randomly sample a token sequence and repeat it. Replace an individual head’s output with its mean activation and measure how the desired copied-token logit changes. Select strongly suppressive heads, freeze the LLM, and learn their scalar coefficients from the second half’s next-token loss. At deployment, multiply the relevant output-projection weights by these fixed scalars, avoiding added runtime operations (§4.1–4.2, Equations 1–3; Figure 2). Thus, “zero overhead” concerns inference after model-specific discovery and optimization, not the cost of preparing the adaptation.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Table 3 evaluates three RAG datasets with Llama2-7B-chat; Table 4 pairs quality with measured inference time and GPU memory on an H800, explicitly without FlashAttention. Table 5 varies the gold-document position in NaturalQuestions-Open-derived multi-document QA and compares three model families using RoPE, learned positional embeddings, and ALiBi. Table 6 checks MMLU to probe collateral changes in knowledge performance. Tables 7–8 vary the number of re-weighted heads. Figure 4 plots learned coefficients, including coefficients above one, revealing that the trained intervention is more complex than uniformly suppressing every chosen head (§5.2–5.6).

[Likely] **What to imitate.** Tie a strong efficiency claim to a concrete algebraic implementation, then test it with a cost table beside the task results. Use a small proxy-task example before introducing discovery machinery. The experiments anticipate natural reviewer objections in order: does it help, does it cost more, does it transfer across architectures, does it damage other capabilities, and what controls it?

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** §4.1 declines to claim one shared suppression mechanism for all detected heads, leaving per-head mechanistic explanation to future work; §6 leaves other applications unevaluated. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** The random-copy proxy’s transfer is empirical, not proof that copying and general RAG are equivalent. MMLU changes from 45.81 to 45.41 in Table 6, so the evidence supports a small reported change on that test, not universal preservation of knowledge. Table 3 does not show the best score on every task, and Table 5 includes a RoPE first-position loss. The categorical headings should be imitated more cautiously than the experimental structure.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Locate a small internal intervention with a testable operational benefit, and explain it through a deliberately simple proxy. Validate downstream transfer, implementation cost, architectural scope, and collateral effects as separate claims.”

## 6. TourRank

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** This is a zero-shot inference-algorithm paper for reranking a fixed candidate set. Its contribution combines seeded grouping, stagewise selection, and points accumulated across multiple tournaments; it is not a new LLM or a learned ranking objective (§3). [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/TourRank_Utilizing_Large_Language_Models_for_Documents_Ranking_with_a_Tournament_Inspired_Stra__2406_11678.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Related Works → Method: TourRank (basic tournament; accumulated points; grouping/selection; complete algorithm) → Experiments (four RQs; setup; main results; initial-order sensitivity; retriever variation; effectiveness/resource tradeoff; other LLMs) → Conclusions → acknowledgments/references → Appendices A–G covering related-work distinctions, round counts, a case, complexity, repeated RankGPT, hyperparameters, and prompts.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** The paper first contrasts pointwise, pairwise, and listwise prompting. It then turns three weaknesses of listwise ranking into explicit numbered requirements: compose a global ranking from local comparisons, parallelize calls, and use the retriever’s initial ranking without depending excessively on it. Only then does the sporting analogy appear. Figure 1’s tournament is mapped point by point to these requirements, followed by the actual grouping and points mechanism (§1, pp. 1–2). The metaphor therefore explains design constraints rather than merely supplying a memorable method name.

[Certain] **Mechanism.** Distribute documents from different initial rank bands into groups, shuffle within groups, and ask the LLM to select documents advancing to the next stage. Each advancement earns a point. Repeat tournaments, sum points, and sort. Groups within a stage and separate tournaments can run concurrently, although advancement stages remain sequential (§3; Figures 2–3; Algorithm 1). Appendix Table 7 makes the recipe concrete: a 100→50→20→10→5→2 progression in the reported configuration. More tournaments increase resolution and cost.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Tables 2–3 evaluate ranking quality on TREC DL 19/20 and eight BEIR datasets; all rerank a retrieved top-100. Figure 4 directly tests the diagnosed failure mode by comparing original, shuffled, and reversed BM25 order. Table 4 changes the first-stage retriever to Contriever and SPLADE++ ED. Table 5 separates sequential dependence from total document exposures; Figure 5 measures cost and latency against quality under an explicit API-rate setting. Appendix Figure 8 compares repeated serial RankGPT with parallel TourRank, addressing whether improvement merely comes from more calls. Appendix Table 10 tests additional open and closed LLMs. These experiments closely mirror the introduction’s requirements.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** Write requirements before naming the solution, and ensure that every requirement has its own experiment. The quality–cost and quality–latency plots are more informative than a single efficiency adjective. Keep theoretical parallel depth, token expenditure, and measured latency distinct. Appendix A.2’s explicit comparison with ListT5 is also useful: clarify the nearest conceptual neighbor using operational differences.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** §4.2 acknowledges weak results on Touche/Signal; §4.6 acknowledges cases where strong-model RankGPT is comparable, and limits costly API runs. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** “Robust” means robust under tested order transformations, not order invariance. Token costs grow with tournament count, and lower latency depends on available parallelism and rate limits. The candidate-set ceiling remains. Figure 5’s historical API costs are experimental context, not current pricing. The reported successes do not establish an optimal tournament design or prove that all ranking bias disappears.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Translate the shortcomings of the leading paradigm into explicit requirements, then use an analogy only where its operations satisfy those requirements. Mirror the requirement list with controlled robustness tests and a measured quality–resource frontier.”

## 7. ED2: Dual Dynamic Indexing for Sequential Recommendation

[Certain] **Contribution and paper type.** ED2 is a joint architecture-and-training paper: dynamic user/item indices, a shared LLM-based recommender, alignment objectives, and auxiliary instruction tasks. The supplied revision is dated September 2026; its argument can be studied, but it should not automatically be treated as the exact 2025 submission. [Supplied PDF](C:/Users/PUSHKAR/Downloads/WWW2025_PDF_Links_and_Downloader/WWW2025_PDFs/Oral_papers/Unleash_LLMs_Potential_for_Sequential_Recommendation_by_Coordinating_Dual_Dynamic_Index_Mechan__2409_09253.pdf).

[Certain] **Macro order.** Introduction → Methodology (problem formulation; dual dynamic recommender; multi-grained token regulator; high-order interaction exploitation) → Experiment (setup; main result; architecture and indexing ablations) → Parameter Sensitivity Analysis (loss weights; learning rate) → Related Work → Conclusion → acknowledgments/references → Appendices A–I with baselines, data, implementation, notation, concurrent-work comparison, complexity, LSH, collisions, and prompts.

[Certain] **Introduction moves.** It moves from sequential recommendation to the need to combine textual semantics with collaborative behavior, then distinguishes content-based and index-based LLM approaches. Two gaps follow: frozen indices cannot adapt to recommendation learning, and omitted user information limits modeled interaction patterns. Figure 1 juxtaposes the conventional and proposed data/gradient routes. The remedy creates two further challenges—understanding changing index tokens and expressing high-order interactions—which motivate the token regulator and auxiliary tasks respectively (§1, pp. 2–3). The introduction therefore distinguishes the architectural idea from the training machinery needed to make it work.

[Certain] **Mechanism.** The shared LLM encodes user/item text; separate hierarchical quantizers map those representations to compact index-token sequences. The extended-vocabulary LLM predicts a next-item index from user and interaction-history indices. Index-level alignment relates an entity’s index representation to its text representation; token-level alignment regulates codeword similarity. Additional tasks predict co-interacting users or model user preferences (§2.2–2.4; Equations 7–11). Figure 2 follows concrete music-product entities through representation, indexing, and generation; Figure 3 locates the two alignment signals.

[Certain] **Evidence jobs.** Table 1 compares recommendation metrics on three Amazon categories—Instruments, Games, and Arts—under a chronological leave-one-out protocol with separate last-item test and penultimate-item validation (§3.1.2–3.1.3). The two ablation tables answer different questions. Table 2 removes user information, index updating, the regulator, or auxiliary exploitation tasks to examine architecture dependencies. Table 3 varies index length and contrasts static/dynamic variants, including LSH, to test whether the dynamic-index idea extends beyond one indexing mechanism. Figure 4 varies alignment weights; Figure 5 varies learning rate. Appendix H addresses collisions and describes an additional token for disambiguation.

[Likely] **What to imitate.** Distinguish “our architecture needs this capability” from “this auxiliary component teaches it.” Use separate ablation families for system composition and the proposed mechanism’s alternatives. The paired `w/o user` and `w/o exploit` variants are more informative than one generic ablation because they probe whether simply supplying user information is sufficient. A worked entity flowing through the pipeline helps readers decode otherwise dense index notation.

[Certain] **Stated boundary.** Appendix H explicitly says hierarchical quantization does not guarantee unique indices and that collision rates depend on scale and configuration. [Likely] **Reviewer boundary.** Three domains from one Amazon source do not establish generality across platforms or cold-start settings. Final recommendation gains support component utility, not direct measurement of what the LLM “understands.” The supplied description does not fully resolve temporal construction of all user-derived auxiliary information; that should be specified before alleging or dismissing leakage. Appendix F discusses Gaussian sampling while the main method specifies von Mises–Fisher sampling, so implementation and complexity exposition need reconciliation before being reused. No dedicated limitations section appears.

[Likely] **Reusable two-sentence pattern.** “Identify a representational disconnect and a missing information source, then show the new architecture that connects both. Treat the difficulties introduced by that architecture as explicit training requirements, and test architectural ingredients separately from mechanism choices.”

## Cross-paper synthesis for writing a Web Conference paper

[Likely] The most valuable commonality is a **chain of obligations**: the introduction names a specific failure, the method adds an operation that responds to it, and the experiments assign that operation a test. The headings themselves vary substantially. LLM4Rerank and ED2 defer related work; MA4DIV and PEAR add background before the method; ED2 makes sensitivity analysis its own major section. Copy the chain, not a supposedly mandatory section order.

| Writing decision | Strong examples in this partition | Practical lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Make the failure visible | HtmlRAG Fig. 1; ED2 Fig. 1 | [Likely] Show exactly what is lost or disconnected before displaying the proposed architecture. |
| Explain why multiple components are needed | HtmlRAG §1; ImageScope §1; ED2 §1 | [Likely] Let each component answer a distinct bottleneck, including bottlenecks created by the main idea itself. |
| Turn aspirations into observable controls | LLM4Rerank Table 3 | [Likely] Evaluate both the requested behavioral change and the computation that actually changes. |
| Test the motivating failure directly | TourRank Fig. 4; PEAR Table 5 | [Likely] Recreate order or position sensitivity rather than relying solely on an aggregate benchmark. |
| Keep gains and costs separate | TourRank Fig. 5; PEAR Tables 3–4; ImageScope Fig. 5 | [Likely] Report cost in the resource dimension the method changes, under explicit conditions. |
| Separate kinds of ablation | ED2 Tables 2–3; HtmlRAG Tables 2–3 | [Likely] Distinguish representation choice, component removal, and alternative mechanisms. |
| Expose intermediate objects | ImageScope Fig. 6; LLM4Rerank Fig. 7; TourRank Appendix Fig. 7 | [Likely] Show what changed between input and final output, while labeling examples and aggregates accurately. |

[Certain] The papers also contain boundaries that should survive any writing imitation: mixed benchmark outcomes, setup-dependent efficiency, inference costs excluded from “training-free” claims, and incompletely demonstrated mechanistic explanations. [Likely] A stronger manuscript preserves the useful architecture of these arguments while narrowing categorical language to the evidence actually reported. Broad adjectives such as “general,” “safe,” “optimal,” “personalized,” or “efficient” should be followed by the condition, metric, or intervention that makes them testable.
