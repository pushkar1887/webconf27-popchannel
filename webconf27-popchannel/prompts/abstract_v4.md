Embedding sharing cuts recommender memory several-fold, but we show that it also erases each item's own popularity, a signal rankers depend on. Prior work showed that full-table recommenders store item popularity in embedding lengths, and that normalization, per-item biases or a log-popularity score term can control it. Recent sharing methods group items by hashing, frequency or user–item graph clustering, and some learn per-item scalars to separate colliding items. Neither line asks what sharing does to the popularity signal. Analyzing it, we find that a shared row's length tracks its bucket's total traffic (Spearman 0.93–0.94), not the item's own popularity (0.28–0.52). We trace a reported anomaly to this pooling: DirectAU outperforms BPR on full tables (Gowalla Recall@20 18.02 vs 16.55) but falls behind it under graph-clustered sharing (7.55 vs 9.37). We prove that no per-bucket quantity explains more than a fraction η² of popularity variance (0.36 on Gowalla); an exact decomposition assigns 71–76% of the recoverable gain to the item-level residual no shared table can store, and 12% to breaking ties. Because DirectAU's loss is scale-invariant, learned per-item scales get zero gradient and cannot hold this residual. We therefore restore it at scoring time, without retraining, by adding β·log(1+count) to cosine scores with one validation-tuned β. Compressed DirectAU on Gowalla rises from 7.55 to 13.30, 42% above the best unrepaired model under the same sharing, and beats equally repaired BPR by 0.88–2.13 points on three datasets, with positive paired-user confidence intervals for every dataset and Gowalla seed. The ordering holds under random and double-frequency hashing; shuffling counts within buckets removes the gain. The float32 prior adds 3.9–7.3% to the compressed table and trades tail recall and coverage for head accuracy.
