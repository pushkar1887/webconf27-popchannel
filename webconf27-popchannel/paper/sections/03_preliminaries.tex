\section{Problem Formulation and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:prelim}

This section fixes the setting in which we study embedding sharing (\S\ref{sec:prelim-setting}). It then separates the training objective from the scoring rule applied after training (\S\ref{sec:prelim-scoring}). Finally, it states the problem that the rest of the paper diagnoses (\S\ref{sec:prelim-problem}).

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Recommendation Setting and Embedding Sharing}
\label{sec:prelim-setting}

We study top-$K$ recommendation from implicit feedback. Users $\mathcal{U}$ and items $\mathcal{I}$ interact through observed pairs, which are split into training, validation and test sets. A model represents user $u$ by $\mathbf{p}_u\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and item $i$ by $\mathbf{q}_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$. For each user, it scores the candidate items and recommends the $K$ highest-scoring items the user has not yet interacted with. The \emph{popularity} of item $i$ is its number of training interactions, $c_i$; validation and test interactions never enter this count. Our primary backbone is matrix factorisation (MF), in which $\mathbf{p}_u$ and $\mathbf{q}_i$ are rows of embedding tables. We also use LightGCN~\cite{LightGCN}, in which they are the outputs of propagation over the training graph.

A full model stores one trainable row for each user and each item. Embedding sharing instead fixes, before training, a many-to-one assignment $h$ from IDs to a much smaller table $\mathbf{E}$. Under single-row sharing,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{q}_i=\mathbf{E}\bigl[h(i)\bigr],
  \label{eq:sharing}
\end{equation}
and likewise for users. The size of the table then depends on the number of rows rather than on the size of the catalog.

We call the set of items mapped to one row a \emph{bucket}, write $b(i)=h(i)$ for the bucket of item $i$, and define the bucket's \emph{traffic} $C_b$ as the total training count of its items. Our main scheme is GraphHash~\cite{GraphHash}. It clusters the bipartite training graph by Louvain modularity and gives each cluster one row. On Gowalla, Yelp2018 and AmazonBook, it yields 5{,}806, 7{,}656 and 9{,}804 item rows, and the shared tables hold 16.4\%, 21.9\% and 13.6\% of the full tables' parameters. We also study three other schemes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Random hashing, with the same row counts as GraphHash;
  \item frequency-based double hashing (DoubleFreq)~\cite{Zhang2020freq}, which gives each item two codes and sums the corresponding rows;
  \item DoubleGraph, which also sums two rows and serves as a structure-aware substitute for BACO~\cite{BACO}.
\end{itemize}

Sharing changes what an item embedding can express. All items in a bucket receive the same vector, so they share a single magnitude $\|\mathbf{q}_i\|$, even when their popularities differ widely. Item popularity and bucket traffic need not move together: a rarely seen item in a busy bucket inherits the magnitude of that bucket. On a full table, magnitude can store popularity item by item. Under single-row sharing, it can store popularity only bucket by bucket.

\subsection{Training Objectives and Scoring Rules}
\label{sec:prelim-scoring}

We compare two objectives that differ in whether they see embedding magnitude.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{BPR}~\cite{BPR} trains on dot products: each observed pair must outscore an unobserved item sampled uniformly at random. Its loss therefore depends on both the direction and the magnitude of the embeddings.
  \item \emph{DirectAU}~\cite{DirectAU} trains on $\ell_2$-normalised embeddings. It combines an alignment term, which pulls interacting users and items together, with a uniformity term, which spreads the representations over the unit sphere; we set the trade-off weight $\gamma$ to 1. Its loss depends only on directions. Magnitudes still change during training, through the optimiser and weight decay, but the loss never asks them to encode anything.
\end{itemize}
Both objectives are trained with Adam.

We separate the \emph{training objective} from the \emph{scoring rule} applied to the trained embeddings. Raw scoring, $\langle\mathbf{p}_u,\mathbf{q}_i\rangle$, uses both magnitude and direction. Cosine scoring uses direction only. Comparing the two on the same checkpoint therefore measures what magnitude contributes to ranking, without changing any learned parameter.

To measure what item-level popularity contributes, we add a log-count prior to either base score:
\begin{equation}
  s_\beta(u,i)=s_{\mathrm{base}}(u,i)+\beta\log(1+c_i),
  \qquad
  s_{\mathrm{base}}\in\bigl\{\langle\mathbf{p}_u,\mathbf{q}_i\rangle,\ \cos(\mathbf{p}_u,\mathbf{q}_i)\bigr\}.
  \label{eq:score}
\end{equation}
This is the score form of Cheng~\cite[Eq.~53]{Cheng2026}, and we call its cosine version the \emph{count-restored score}. It is applied after training, stores one scalar per item, and serves here as a diagnostic probe rather than a proposed method. The base score of each objective is fixed in advance: cosine for DirectAU, which is trained on normalised embeddings, and raw for BPR, which is trained on dot products. Only $\beta$ is selected, by validation Recall@20, and the test split is scored once.

\subsection{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:prelim-problem}

Table~\ref{tab:problem} shows the behaviour that motivates this study. Earlier work reported it without a mechanism. GraphHash~\cite{GraphHash} found that hashing methods lose accuracy when the objective changes from BPR to DirectAU. Shiao et al.~\cite{Shiao2025} found BPR ahead of DirectAU for out-of-vocabulary users represented by hashed embeddings. In our controlled setting, both objectives use the same data, backbone, embedding dimension and sharing assignment, and therefore the same parameter budget.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{Raw test Recall@20 (\%). The last two columns give the cost of sharing, i.e.\ full minus GraphHash, in percentage points. Sharing costs DirectAU more than BPR in every setting.}
  \label{tab:problem}
  \small
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Full} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{GraphHash} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{Cost of sharing} \\
    \cmidrule(lr){2-3}\cmidrule(lr){4-5}\cmidrule(l){6-7}
    Setting & BPR & DAU & BPR & DAU & BPR & DAU \\
    \midrule
    MF, Gowalla       & 16.55 & 18.02 & 9.37  & 7.55  & 7.18 & \textbf{10.47} \\
    MF, Yelp2018      &  8.02 & 11.10 & 3.78  & 2.41  & 4.24 & \textbf{8.69} \\
    MF, AmazonBook    &  8.38 &  9.57 & 4.10  & 4.63  & 4.28 & \textbf{4.94} \\
    \midrule
    LightGCN, Gowalla & 18.48 & 18.02 & 16.22 & 11.29 & 2.26 & \textbf{6.73} \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two patterns stand out.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Sharing costs DirectAU more than BPR in every setting}, by a factor of 1.15 to 3.0.
  \item \textbf{Where DirectAU leads on full tables, this asymmetry is large enough to reverse the order.} The order reverses on Gowalla and Yelp2018. On AmazonBook, where the asymmetry is smallest, DirectAU's lead only narrows, from 1.19 to 0.53 points. With LightGCN, BPR already leads on the full Gowalla table, so DirectAU has no lead to lose, but sharing widens BPR's margin from 0.46 to 4.93 points.
\end{enumerate}
The reversal is therefore a visible consequence. The asymmetric cost is the phenomenon to explain.

\medskip
\noindent\fbox{\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}{%
\textbf{Problem.} Given a fixed sharing assignment and fixed training and evaluation protocols, why does embedding sharing cost DirectAU more than BPR? We answer three questions.
\begin{description}
  \item[Q1.] What item-level popularity information can a shared table represent, and what does it lose?
  \item[Q2.] Does losing that information account for DirectAU's larger cost? The alternatives are reduced directional capacity, ties within buckets, and training artefacts.
  \item[Q3.] What does restoring item-level popularity at scoring time recover, and what does it cost in storage, tail recall and catalog coverage?
\end{description}}}
\medskip

The representational analysis is exact for single-row sharing in MF, where all items in a bucket share one vector. Two-row schemes give an item the sum of two rows. LightGCN propagates each item over its own neighbourhood, so items that share a row need not end with identical embeddings. We therefore treat these settings as tests of how far the diagnosis extends. Section~\ref{sec:method} turns Q1--Q3 into hypotheses and tests.
